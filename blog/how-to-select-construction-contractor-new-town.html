Hiring a Contractor

How to Select a Construction Contractor in New Town

Weston Engineers • Kolkata • 7 min read

New Town and Rajarhat have had a steady pipeline of residential and commercial construction for years now, which means there's no shortage of contractors quoting for your project. The problem isn't finding one — it's telling the ones who'll deliver from the ones who'll disappear at the finishing stage. Here's what actually separates them.

Ask to See a Real, Recent Site

Not a finished, photographed showpiece — a site that's currently mid-construction. A contractor confident in their site discipline won't mind you walking an active project. One who stalls on this, or only offers to show completed work, is telling you something.

When you're there, look past the finishes. Check whether the site is organized, whether safety basics are visible, and whether there's someone who can answer specific technical questions about the stage of work in progress — not just a site supervisor reciting a sales pitch.

Get Everything in a BOQ, Not Just a Quote

A single lump-sum number is the easiest way for a contract to become a dispute later. Ask for an itemized Bill of Quantities — broken down by material, quantity, and rate — before you sign anything. If a contractor resists itemizing, that's usually because a lump sum gives them room to substitute cheaper materials without it being visible in the paperwork.

Clarify Who's Actually Supervising, Day to Day

The person who wins the contract and the person who's actually on site every day are often different people. Ask specifically: who is the site engineer, how often are they physically present, and what's the escalation path if something goes wrong. A contractor who can't answer this concretely is likely running the site reactively, not proactively.

The best time to find out a contractor cuts corners is before you sign, not three months into a foundation pour.

Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

  • A quote significantly below every other bid — without a clear, specific reason (bulk material sourcing, in-house labor). Someone is subsidizing that number, and it's usually quality or a mid-project renegotiation.
  • No written payment schedule tied to milestones — payment should follow verified progress, not a calendar.
  • Reluctance to allow a third-party site inspection — whether that's your own engineer or an independent PMC.
  • Vague answers on structural specifications — steel grade, concrete mix ratio, foundation depth should be answerable precisely, not approximately.

Consider an Independent Check, Even If You Trust the Contractor

Trusting a contractor and verifying their work aren't in conflict — they're both necessary. An independent site supervisor or PMC engagement costs a fraction of what a single serious construction defect costs to fix after the fact, and it gives you someone accountable to you, not to the contractor's own profit margin.
