Site Supervision & QA/QC: What You Should Expect During Construction

Weston Engineers • Kolkata • 6 min read

"We do quality checks" is something every contractor will tell you. What that means in practice varies enormously — from a genuine documented process to a supervisor eyeballing the site once a week. Here's what proper site supervision and QA/QC actually look like, so you know what to ask for.

Site Supervision: Presence, Not Just Inspection

Supervision means someone independent is on site regularly enough to catch problems while they're still fixable — not just walking through after a stage is complete. On an active project this typically means:

  • Scheduled site visits tied to construction milestones, not a fixed calendar that might land between the two moments that actually mattered.
  • Photographic records of work at each stage, especially anything that gets covered by the next stage — rebar before concrete, plumbing before it's boxed in.
  • Drawing compliance checks — confirming what's being built matches the approved structural and architectural drawings, not a modified version the site team found easier to execute.

QA/QC: The Difference Between the Two

Quality Assurance (QA) is process — the procedures in place to prevent defects before they happen: material testing protocols, approved vendor lists, checklists for each construction stage. Quality Control (QC) is inspection — catching defects that occur despite QA, through testing and physical checks against specification.

A project with strong QC but no QA catches problems late, after material is already on site or work is already done. A project with strong QA but no QC has good intentions with no verification that they were followed. You need both.

What Should Actually Be Documented

  • Material test reports — concrete cube test results, steel mill certificates, not just a verbal assurance that "good quality" material was used.
  • Stage-wise inspection checklists — signed off before proceeding to the next stage, so there's a record of who approved what and when.
  • Non-conformance reports — a record of anything that didn't meet spec and how it was resolved, not just silence when something goes wrong.
  • Daily or weekly site progress reports — manpower, material delivered, work completed, so schedule slippage is visible early rather than discovered at handover.
If you ask for material test reports or inspection checklists and get a shrug instead of a folder, that's the answer to whether QA/QC is actually happening.

Why This Matters More Than It Seems

Structural defects, water seepage, and finishing issues are expensive precisely because they're discovered after the work is covered up — by the time a crack or a leak shows itself, the fix means undoing finished work, not correcting an open checklist item. Proper site supervision and QA/QC exist to move that discovery point earlier, when it's still a line item instead of a rebuild.
